The recommendation

Use a targeted mix. Make the evidence mandatory.

Repair drainage and foundations first. Use flexible asphalt where access, repairability and initial cost matter; consider jointed concrete at stable, heavily stressed locations. Choose each segment through a published whole-life comparison.

Our preferred policy architecture is a site-specific portfolio. The example’s 70% asphalt / 30% concrete split is an assumption to test, not a national quota or an optimized design.

Verified policy detail

“If technically appropriate” matters.

The official directive, §5(3), conditions domestic pavement alternatives on technical suitability. §5(4) requires justification when foreign materials or technology are needed. A blanket material substitution would miss that distinction.

Existing engineering guidance

Nepal has both design pathways.

DoR has flexible and rigid pavement design guidelines (2021), plus concrete-pavement provisions in its amended specifications. These are design tools; they do not establish one material as the economic winner across Nepal.

First principles

A road is a system.
The surface is only one part.

Its job is to spread repeated wheel loads, shed water and keep a route usable. Failure begins when demand exceeds the capacity of any layer—or the ground beneath it.

Water

Drain it before it weakens support.

Rain entering cracks or saturated foundations reduces support. Concrete still needs drainage: eroded support can produce pumping, faulting and slab cracking.

Load

Measure axles, not just vehicle counts.

Heavy axle repetitions govern structural demand. Weigh freight traffic and test subgrade strength; a busy bus stop differs from a lightly used rural link.

Ground

Keep the road where the ground stays.

Neither surface fixes landslides, river scour or ongoing settlement. Stabilize, redesign drainage or realign before selecting pavement.

Delivery

Construction quality is part of design.

Asphalt needs controlled mix temperatures and density. Concrete needs joints, load transfer, curing and verified strength. Both need accountable maintenance.

Why these architectures—and what else belongs in the toolbox?

Jointed plain concrete is a practical first pilot: it uses established DoR guidance and avoids making continuous reinforcement, precast systems or bonded overlays the default. Roller-compacted concrete and overlays need their own equipment, interface and performance trials. Surface dressing, gravel, stone and block paving can be better for low-volume or short access roads; their service level differs from this 7 m, two-lane corridor example. Do not use this comparison to require costly asphalt or concrete on every rural road.

A realistic implementation plan

Start with evidence.
Earn the right to scale.

A 36-month learning programme, followed by longer monitoring. Work around monsoon seasons and demonstrate construction quality before committing to a network-wide material preference.

Months 0–3

Publish the decision rules

MoPIT/DoR convenes provincial and local road owners, independent engineers and laboratories. Publish the appraisal method, conflict disclosures, data template and eligibility gates.

Gate: safe drainage outlets, stable ground and maintainable access.

Months 3–6

Survey and design a matched pilot

Select three environments: Terai freight, an urban utility corridor and stable hill-road sections. Measure axle loading, wet-season subgrade, drainage and materials. In each environment compare three matched pairs, each with 0.5 km asphalt and 0.5 km concrete: 9 km total.

Gate: equivalent structural service, diversion feasibility and an independent bill-of-quantities review.

Months 6–15

Procure, train and build in dry windows

Use the applicable procurement route; train crews on trial strips, density testing, joints and curing. Allow about 6–10 weeks per asphalt kilometre and 10–16 weeks per concrete kilometre after access is ready, with parallel crews where practical. These are planning allowances.

Gate: accepted foundations, material tests, as-built thickness and signed opening release. Concrete: ≥7 days from dry lean concrete to slab, then no vehicle traffic within 28 days of slab placement.

Months 15–36

Measure two monsoons, then review

Track roughness, rutting, cracks, joint faulting, drainage uptime, maintenance spending and closure hours. Inspect quarterly and after major storms. Extend the programme if the completion date does not leave two full monsoons before review.

Gate: publish costs and performance by site. Scale only where the comparison supports it; continue annual monitoring for at least 5–10 years.

Who makes it work?

Road owner: accountable for design acceptance, outlets, maintenance and public reporting.

DoR and independent reviewers: set equivalent design and testing requirements; publish the comparisons.

Contractors and accredited/capable labs: verify mix, thickness, density or flexural strength, joint alignment and curing records.

Roads Board / responsible budget authority: seek a ring-fenced, approved maintenance allocation. No funding commitment is assumed here.

What exactly should trigger scale-up—or a stop?

Before tender, define project-specific acceptance thresholds for roughness, rut depth, flexural strength, density, joint faulting and defect liability under the adopted specifications. Proposed management targets: 100% documented acceptance tests and outlets inspected before handover; no unapproved opening; at least 95% of scheduled inspections completed; all critical drainage blockages triaged within 48 hours. These are proposed programme targets, not existing Nepal legal thresholds. A safety defect, unstable ground, systematic QA failure or unfunded maintenance stops scale-up. A two-monsoon pilot validates delivery and early performance; it cannot establish 30-year pavement life.

What this study can—and cannot—conclude

It supports transparent, site-specific selection and a measured pilot. It cannot confirm a universal national cost saving, establish the best concrete share without surveys, certify a construction design or determine a carbon winner without a comparable life-cycle inventory. Domestic cement still uses energy and may use imported fuels or other inputs; asphalt also contains substantial local aggregate and labour. Foreign-exchange benefit must be calculated from actual imported content, not gross project cost.
