# Nepal road decisions: choose the pavement for the place

Research cutoff: **18 October 2026**. Delivered: **19 October 2026**. Independent screening study; no government funding, construction design or procurement approval is implied. Currency is Nepalese rupees: **NPR 10 million = 1 crore**.

## Recommendation and reading of the statement

Nepal should use a **segment-specific pavement programme**, repairing drainage and foundations first, then choosing engineered asphalt or jointed concrete through a disclosed whole-life comparison. Concrete deserves serious consideration at suitable, stable, heavily stressed locations. Asphalt remains attractive where initial affordability, utility access, staged upgrading and short closures matter. The preferred policy can legitimately select either material for an entire corridor if the evidence supports it.

Surya Raj Acharya's attached statement makes a sound methodological demand: support domestic production, but require technical and economic evidence before deciding road materials. The supplied screenshot is the basis for this reading; the linked X page could not be independently retrieved. Nothing reviewed establishes improper industry influence.

The actual policy is more conditional than its headline. Section 5(3) of the **Public Procurement Use and Promotion of Domestic Products Directive, 2083** makes the pavement preference conditional on technical suitability. Section 5(4) requires justification for foreign materials or technology; other clauses address quality, availability and documented exceptions. The useful next step is to make this suitability test measurable and public. This is an analytical reading, not an official translation. [Official directive, pages 2–3](https://giwmscdnone.gov.np/media/pdf_upload/IMG_0002_a7yupar.pdf).

Official design methods and some Nepal comparison studies exist. **A published nationwide comparative appraisal specifically supporting the October 2026 directive was not located in the sources reviewed.** That bounded search finding does not prove that no report exists. The responsible agencies should publish any appraisal, its assumptions and underlying data.

## First principles: what must the road accomplish?

A road must distribute repeated wheel loads, shed water and provide dependable access. Its surface is only one part of that system.

**Loads:** axle weight and repetitions determine structural demand. Asphalt spreads load through several layers; concrete carries it through slab action and joint load transfer. Counting vehicles without understanding heavy axles can misstate demand.

**Water and support:** wet foundations can lose strength. Concrete can also fail when water and wheel movement erode support beneath slabs. Neither surface solves a landslide, washed-out embankment or blocked outlet. Nepal's Narayanghat–Mugling work combines drainage, slope engineering and bioengineering. [FHWA pavement drainage mechanisms](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/engineering/geotech/pubs/05037/07a.cfm); [World Bank Nepal slope example](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/feature/2025/02/10/protecting-slopes-to-build-resilient-roads-in-nepal).

**Time and quality:** asphalt needs suitable mixes, compaction and timely renewal; concrete needs controlled batching, joints, curing and repairs. A maintenance-free life cannot be inferred from the material name.

Two illustrative calculations show why site inputs matter:

- With 1,000 commercial vehicles/day, 50% assigned to the design lane, 3 equivalent standard axles/vehicle and 20 years, cumulative loading is `365 × 1000 × 0.5 × 3 × ((1+g)^20−1)/g`. At annual growth of 3%, 5% and 7%, demand is **14.71, 18.10 and 22.45 million standard axles**. These are assumed scenarios, not surveyed Nepal traffic.
- A 1 km × 7 m carriageway has 7,000 m² area. At assumed rainfall intensity 100 mm/hour and runoff coefficient 0.8, `Q = C × i × A = 0.156 m³/second`, before adding uphill catchments. A drain must have a safe, maintained outlet. This calculation is not hydraulic sizing.

## Three solutions and their architectures

| Solution | Construction and operation | Best candidates | Principal tradeoff |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Drainage-first flexible asphalt** | Restore outlets/support; construct designed foundations and asphalt layers; inspect, seal, patch and resurface on time. | Ordinary corridors, towns with utility work, staged strengthening, routes needing short closures. | Recurring renewals and binder-price exposure; poor compaction or drainage can erase value. |
| **B. Engineered jointed plain concrete** | Prepared support, drainage/filter layer, lean-concrete subbase, separation membrane, designed slabs, load-transfer joints, texture and curing. | Stable freight junctions, bus stopping areas and other demonstrated heavy-load locations with viable traffic arrangements. | Higher initial expense in this model, specialist quality control, difficult utility cuts and longer opening restrictions. |
| **C. Targeted corridor portfolio** | Select A or B for each homogeneous segment; engineer transitions and coordinate utilities, drainage and maintenance. | Programmes spanning different terrain, traffic and settlement conditions. | Requires good diagnosis and competence in both systems; poor segmentation adds complexity without benefit. |

These are **two construction systems and one deployment strategy**, not an exhaustive list of pavement materials. C combines adjacent sections; it is not an arbitrary concrete/asphalt sandwich. Its illustrative 70/30 allocation is neither an optimized share nor a national quota.

Use established jointed concrete as the first concrete comparator. Continuously reinforced, precast and roller-compacted systems introduce additional equipment or specialist requirements. Cement-treated bases beneath asphalt are also credible candidates: Nepal has dedicated 2024 guidance, but mix design, curing and reflection-crack control still require verification. Surface dressing, gravel, stone and block paving deserve separate appraisal on low-volume/access roads. [DoR CTB/CTSB guideline](https://dor.gov.np/home/publication/guide-to-road-slope-and-protection/force/26-dor-pbl-gdl-2024-026-cement-treated-sub-base-base-guideline--dor-2024-final).

Nepal already has [flexible](https://dor.gov.np/qrdc/publication/guidelines-and-manualsguidelines-and-manuals/force/guidelines-for-the-design-of-flexible-pavement-2-21) and [rigid](https://dor.gov.np/qrdc/publication/guidelines-and-manualsguidelines-and-manuals/force/rigid-pavement-guideline-2-21) pavement design guidelines. Use measured axle loads, wet subgrade strength, local materials and temperature conditions to design both alternatives to equivalent service. The section quantities below are costing examples, not completed structural designs.

## Nepal's resources: real advantages, measured carefully

Domestic cement production creates a useful supply option. Customs' preliminary FY 2024/25 workbook records **356,473 tonnes of non-white Portland cement exports**. It also records **158,507 tonnes of petroleum-bitumen imports**, valued at **NPR 11.413 billion**, with **94.74% by mass from India**. These historical trade statistics indicate manufacturing capability and import exposure; they are not 2026 delivered construction prices or road-only consumption. [Department of Customs annual workbook](https://giwmscdnone.gov.np/media/files/FTS_Annual_2081_82_twybrul.xlsx).

Domestic supply is not automatically import-free: fuel, equipment, steel and some inputs can still be imported. Asphalt also uses local aggregate, labour and services. Calculate actual imported content and domestic value added; do not classify the entire asphalt contract as an import or the entire concrete contract as an economic saving.

Use DoR/QRDC engineering capacity, capable local contractors, independent laboratories and road-owner maintenance teams. Plant proximity, material certification, haul distance, water availability and repair capability matter more than nominal national capacity.

## Transparent cost calculation

The demonstration is **10 km of existing, stable, accessible alignment**, with a 7 m carriageway and 1.5 m total shoulder width outside it. It contains 70% ordinary and 30% stressed segments. All installed rates and maintenance intervals are **analyst assumptions**, not verified bids.

Per kilometre, common formation, 200 mm granular subbase, drains/outlets, shoulders, safety and contractor QA cost **NPR 14.10 million**. Asphalt adds 200 mm wet-mix macadam, 60 mm dense bituminous macadam, 40 mm wearing course and coats: **NPR 19.46 million**. Concrete adds 150 mm lean concrete, a 250 mm slab, joints/dowels/ties and membrane: **NPR 37.94 million**.

Quantity equals area × thickness. For example, lean concrete is `7000 × 0.15 = 1050 m³/km`; at assumed NPR 9,000/m³, its cost is **NPR 9.45 million/km**. The 150 mm example follows DoR's major-highway DLC recommendation; it does not establish the adequacy of the full pavement.

| Ten-kilometre demonstration, NPR crore | A: asphalt | B: concrete | C: 70/30 portfolio |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Direct works | 33.56 | 52.04 | 39.25 |
| Base capital including 5% design/supervision | 35.24 | 54.64 | 41.22 |
| Initial cash including 15% contingency and 13% VAT reserve | **45.79** | **71.01** | **53.56** |
| Discounted 30-year agency cost | **55.31** | **60.70** | **53.98** |

C includes NPR 1.5 million of transition works over the 10 km. The cash budget and discounted cost measure different things: **do not add them**. Cash includes contingency and a VAT reserve; agency life-cycle cost excludes those, includes design/supervision and maintenance, and deducts residual service value.

Use `PV = initial cost + Σ(cost_t/(1+r)^t) − residual_T/(1+r)^T`, with a **5% real discount rate** here. Ordinary asphalt renewal is assumed every eight years; stressed asphalt every four. Concrete receives joint resealing every five years before year 30, and localized slab repairs at years 15 and 25. Common drainage upkeep is included. [FHWA life-cycle method](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/pavement/lcca/lccafact/).

The model assumes initial structures serve 30 years and credits unused service from the final asphalt renewal. This residual convention is uncertain. User delays, vehicle operating costs, carbon and catastrophic reconstruction are not monetized; this is not a complete social cost-benefit analysis.

**The key sensitivity reverses the choice:** at four-year renewal on stressed sections, concrete's modeled advantage is about NPR 4.97 million/km. At **five-year** renewal, asphalt is cheaper by about **NPR 0.76 million/km**. Better asphalt mix or workmanship may therefore remove the rationale for concrete at a particular site. C's baseline advantage over A is only **NPR 1.33 crore over 10 km**; its modeled sustained discounted crossing occurs in year 20, not a guaranteed payback.

Across 243 deterministic combinations of prices, discount rates, stressed share and renewal intervals, A wins 106 cases, B 45 and C 26, with 66 ties. These are sensitivity cases, **not probabilities**.

## Selection matrix: merit depends on requirements

Before scoring, reject options that fail drainage, ground stability, safety, material quality or traffic-access requirements. A high domestic-content score cannot compensate for an unsafe foundation.

Higher is better. The first two scores are cost ratios; other scores and all weights are judgments. Environmental scores are neutral because comparable inventories are missing. C wins the balanced case because the assumed corridor varies. Budget/access priorities select A; domestic-supply/maintenance priorities select B. Reducing corridor-fit weight to 5% or less also selects A. This makes C a conditional policy recommendation, not a proven universal material advantage.

## Construction time and a practical 36-month programme

Productive paving days differ from project duration. At assumed 500 tonnes/day and asphalt density 2.35 t/m³, the 10 km asphalt option requires **16,450 tonnes / 500 = 32.9 productive days**. At 150 m³/day, concrete slabs require **17,500 / 150 = 116.7 productive days**. Four drain crews each completing 50 m/day need **100 productive days** for 20 km of drains. Preparation, weather, haul, approvals and sequencing add elapsed time.

Current DoR specifications require at least **7 days between DLC and slab placement** and prohibit vehicle traffic on new concrete pavement **within 28 days**. Joints/shoulders and written opening approval also matter. Asphalt needs cooling to ambient and approval. Concrete trials must precede main paving; a strength result alone does not waive the opening clause. [DoR 2082 specifications, clauses 1308(5), 1309(5), 3201(6), 3202(11–12)](https://dor.gov.np/qrdc/publication/qrdc-guidelines-standard-specifications-codes-and-norms/force/standard-specifications-for-road-and-bridge-works-3rd-amendment-2082).

The proposed **9 km experiment** is separate from the 10 km economic demonstration: three environments—Terai freight, urban/peri-urban access and stable hill sections—each contain three matched A/B pairs, with 500 m per treatment. That is **4.5 km of each material**. Randomize order where feasible and match loading, support and drainage. Replication improves the comparison, but baseline variance and statistical power must still be checked.

| Months | Actions and accountable parties | Release gate |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 | MoPIT/DoR, participating road owners and QRDC appoint independent reviewers; publish protocol, disclosures and data rules. | Agreed service requirements and transparent decision criteria. |
| 3–6 | Owner/consultant surveys axles, wet support, drainage, materials and utilities; obtains delivered quotes and designs equivalent alternatives. | Feasible sites, traffic plan, reviewed BOQ and approved maintenance funding. |
| 6–15 | Procure, train and construct in suitable dry windows; contractor demonstrates trials; supervision and independent labs witness hold points. | Accepted foundations, materials, thickness, joints, curing and signed opening release. |
| 15–36 | Road owners maintain sections; independent team inspects quarterly and after major storms, publishes results and revises costs. | At least two post-construction monsoons and independent review before conditional scaling. |

Extend monitoring if seasonal timing prevents two monsoons. Record axle loads, roughness, rutting, cracks, joint faulting, drainage failures, repairs and closure hours. Publish thresholds before tender. Safety-critical defects, systematic QA failures or unfunded maintenance stop scaling. Continue 5–10-year follow-up; two monsoons cannot prove 30-year life.
