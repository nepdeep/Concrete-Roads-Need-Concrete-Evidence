# Pavement choice in Nepal: engineering evidence and pilot design

Research date: 18 October 2026. This is a screening study, not a pavement design certificate or an approved government programme. Source IDs map to `sources-engineering.json`. All proposed pilot targets, lengths, durations and illustrative calculations below are author assumptions unless explicitly attributed.

## What the statement gets right

The attached statement asks whether a comparative technical and economic study supports a reported preference for cement roads. That is the correct decision question: domestic manufacture can be valuable without being sufficient proof that every road should use concrete. Technical specifications already exist for both materials. Their existence is different from a disclosed, corridor-specific economic appraisal of a material-preference policy. This engineering review does not establish that no such policy study exists.

“Cement versus bitumen” is shorthand. Concrete pavement is aggregate bound with cement; asphalt is aggregate bound with bitumen. Both need a suitable foundation, drainage, workmanship, maintenance and traffic control. The relevant product is a dependable road service over time, not tonnes of one binder sold.

## First principles

1. A wheel puts a concentrated force on the surface. The pavement must distribute repeated loads so that its layers and the soil beneath do not accumulate unacceptable deformation or cracks. The number and weight of commercial axles matter more to structural demand than vehicle count alone.
2. Water changes the problem. Saturation can weaken support; wheel movement can then displace wet fine material. Water in asphalt can damage bonding, while water and erodible support can produce pumping and uneven joints beneath concrete. A sound top surface cannot compensate indefinitely for a saturated or moving foundation. [E4]
3. Asphalt deforms with load, time and temperature. Layers must limit subgrade compression and asphalt tensile strain. Concrete spreads loads through slab action but must accommodate shrinkage and temperature-driven movement. Joints, load transfer and uniform support therefore matter alongside compressive strength. [E2, E3]
4. A landslide, washed-out embankment or blocked culvert can close either pavement. Material preference does not resolve catchment hydrology, unstable slopes, river erosion, drainage outlet ownership or maintenance access. Nepal's Narayanghat–Mugling experience combines drainage, bioengineering and engineered slope measures rather than relying on surfacing alone. [E5]
5. The practical objective is to minimise discounted agency and user cost at a required safety and service level, subject to an affordable initial budget, feasible construction and acceptable environmental impacts. Costs must include repair and work-zone delay, not just initial paving. [E6]

### Two transparent calculations

**Traffic loading illustration, not Nepal survey data.** For 1,000 commercial vehicles/day in both directions, 5% annual growth, 50% assigned to the design lane, 3 equivalent standard axles/vehicle and 20 years, cumulative demand is:

`N = 365 × 1000 × 0.50 × 3 × ((1.05^20 − 1)/0.05) = 18.10 million standard axles.`

At 3%, 5% and 7% growth this becomes approximately 14.71, 18.10 and 22.45 msa. These are arithmetic scenarios; survey axle loads, directional distribution, lane allocation and growth before design. A car-count-only policy misses the load variable.

**Drainage illustration, not a drain design.** A 1 km × 7 m carriageway has 7,000 m² area. With assumed runoff coefficient 0.8 and rainfall intensity 100 mm/hour, `Q = C × i × A = 0.8 × (0.1/3600) × 7000 = 0.156 m³/s` from the carriageway alone. A hillside catchment may contribute much more. Road camber moves water to drains; functioning outlets must carry it away without undermining slopes or flooding neighbours. Site intensity-duration-frequency data, catchments, blockage allowance and downstream checks are required before sizing.

## The three actionable solutions

| Option | Architecture and method | Strongest candidates | Main limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Drainage-first flexible pavement | Restore drains and support; engineer granular or justified stabilised foundation; use designed asphalt binder/wearing layers; fund timely sealing, patching and resurfacing. | Existing flexible corridors with repairable foundations; traffic-sensitive urban routes; routes with expected utility openings; staged strengthening. | Binder supply and haul temperature; rutting or moisture damage if mix/compaction is poor; periodic maintenance must be funded. |
| B. Selective jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) | Prepared support, drainage/filter layer, suitable non-erodible subbase, separation layer, jointed slabs; designed dowels/tie bars, saw-cut joints, texture and cure. | Stable, heavily loaded stopping/turning areas; industrial access or bus bays; selected wet or steep sections where drainage and foundation risks can actually be controlled. | Longer closure; specialist batching, joint and curing QA; utility trenching and slab repair are disruptive; settlement/erosion can still cause failure. |
| C. Segment-based mixed programme | Choose A or B for each homogeneous road segment through one published appraisal; design transitions; coordinate drainage, utility access, QA and maintenance as a corridor system. This means adjacent sections, not an untested asphalt/concrete sandwich. | A national or provincial programme spanning urban, hill and Terai conditions. | Requires sound asset data, honest comparison and management of two construction/repair capabilities; short fragmented concrete patches can create poor transitions. |

**Recommended architecture: C, with A as the operational default where its appraisal wins and B at justified sites.** C has the strongest policy merit because conditions vary, while A or B remains the actual construction system at any location. This is an inference, not a measured claim that C has a lower cost everywhere. The appraisal must allow an entire corridor to select one material if that is the result; it must not impose an arbitrary concrete quota.

All alternatives must meet the same road width, axle demand, drainage, safety, roughness and reliability requirement. Do not credit concrete with good drainage while comparing it against poorly drained asphalt. Conversely, do not give asphalt a maintenance programme while assuming concrete receives none.

### How to select a segment

First apply non-compensatory gates. Do not pave over unresolved slope instability, unacceptable settlement, inadequate drainage, inaccessible utilities, inadequate aggregate quality or an infeasible traffic diversion. A good weighted score cannot offset failure of a safety or constructability gate.

Then compare feasible designs by life-cycle agency cost, user delay, reliability in wet conditions, initial affordability, local supply exposure, construction quality capability, future repairs/utilities, and measured whole-life emissions. Publish units, weights, ratings and evidence separately. Local-content benefit is a legitimate criterion; avoid counting the same import saving again in both price and an economic-benefit total.

Use the same analysis horizon and discount basis. Calculate `PV = initial cost + Σ(cost_t/(1+r)^t) − residual value/(1+r)^T`. Separate agency expenditure from user delay and environmental measures. Examine shorter/longer maintenance intervals, discount rates, binder/cement/fuel prices, traffic delays and different residual-service assumptions. A 30-year screening model needs a 40-year sensitivity or an explicit explanation of residual life; never call design life a maintenance-free warranty. [E6, E7]

### Design and method decisions

- Use the 2021 Nepal flexible guideline, not its withdrawn 2014 predecessor. It provides layered design and a pavement-selection framework, including local resources and construction constraints. Its low-volume and surface-dressing provisions remain relevant outside the medium/heavy-road comparison here. [E2]
- For heavy-road concrete, begin with JPCP as the evaluated candidate rather than continuously reinforced or precast systems with higher specialist requirements. Final thickness must be checked using axle spectra, support, flexural strength and temperature conditions; nominal “M30/M40” labels alone do not establish a satisfactory slab. Nepal's rigid guide recommends at least 150 mm DLC for major highways and direct flexural testing for major projects. [E3]
- Consider warm-mix asphalt and reclaimed asphalt only as qualified variants of A after plant, recovered-material and moisture-performance trials. Lower production temperatures and recycling offer potential benefits, but foreign percentages are not Nepal guarantees. [E8, E9]
- Surface dressing, gravel and targeted spot improvement can deliver better affordability on low-volume access roads. They are not dismissed as inferior; they need their own traffic/service comparison rather than being scored as equivalent to a medium/heavy paved corridor. [E2]
- Do not adopt roller-compacted concrete, high-RAP, plastic-modified asphalt or rapid-opening proprietary mixes as the baseline without local mix verification, equipment demonstration, maintainability and a separate trial. Additive novelty is unnecessary to test the principal policy question.

## Quality and actual construction windows

The **current DoR 2082 specifications** include concrete pavement in Section 3200. Concrete trials must precede paving; a method statement is due one month before the trial and the trial one month before main paving. DLC must wait at least 7 days before slab placement. Concrete slab curing includes at least 14 days of continuously moist hessian, and **traffic is prohibited within 28 days of construction**; joints and shoulders must be complete and the Engineer must authorise opening in writing. Asphalt-concrete opening follows DBM requirements: cool to ambient and obtain written approval. These are different from a promise to finish an entire road in those periods. [E1, clauses 3201(6)(a), 3202(9)(l), 3202(11), 3202(12), 1308(5), 1309(5)]

For programme planning, allow 8–12 weeks before first new-system paving for materials/mix approval, equipment mobilisation and trials; overlap only genuinely independent tasks. Quantity/productivity planning for one already-prepared 1 km × 7 m module might assume 350 m² of accepted surface/day: 20 working paving days. Asphalt closure of each completed zone may end after cooling/acceptance; the final concrete zone still needs its full 28-day opening restriction. Drainage, utilities, earthworks, the DLC sequence, holidays and weather are additional. These are transparent productivity assumptions, not observed Nepal contractor rates or tender durations. Half-width construction preserves some access but can lengthen the work programme and queue delays.

Common inspection hold points: approve material sources and drainage outlets; accept subgrade strength/compaction and finished levels; accept foundations before covering; record production and delivery; confirm thickness, surface regularity, texture and safety before acceptance. Asphalt needs binder/mix verification, temperatures, density and joints; concrete needs batch water control, beams, slab thickness, dowel alignment, saw-cut timing, texture and curing records. Independent checks should be selected by the owner, not only the material supplier. [E1–E3]

## A realistic 36-month evidence programme

This proposed programme is conditional on agency funding and approvals; it is not an existing funded project. Start the clock at mobilisation. A 36-month project can observe construction and early wet-season performance; it cannot prove a 30–40-year lifetime. The target is two post-construction monsoons, not merely 36 elapsed months. If procurement or construction misses the necessary season, extend monitoring rather than lowering the evidence requirement.

**Test network:** three environmental blocks: (1) hot Terai freight/urban lowland, (2) hill corridor with controlled slope and drainage conditions, (3) urban/peri-urban route with stopping loads and coordinated utilities. In each block, construct three comparable A/B matched pairs, each treatment section 500 m long: 3 blocks × 3 pairs × 2 materials × 0.5 km = **9 km** of experimental road. Buffer/transition lengths and off-road construction trial sections are additional. Randomise treatment order where feasible; match geometry, subgrade, drain standard and traffic exposure. Document deviations. The same traffic should pass each paired section where possible. C is the decision rule derived from these comparisons, not a third surface material needing its own incomparable experimental section. This is a feasible screening sample, not a power-justified national experiment. Use baseline variance to check power and revise replication before tender; report block-specific effects and confidence intervals rather than treating many measurements on one section as independent roads.

| Months | Work and responsibility proposed | Gate/output |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 | MoPIT/DoR sponsor; provincial/local road owners join for their assets. QRDC prepares protocol with an independent university/laboratory review team; utilities and traffic police help select feasible sites. | G0: publish study question, conflicts of interest, sample design, baseline measures and data policy. |
| 3–6 | Owner/consultant surveys axles, soaked support, drainage and flood/slope exposure; sample aggregates, cement, binder and water; obtain delivered quotes. | G1: comparable feasible designs, environmental/social requirements, traffic-management and maintenance funding agreed before tender. |
| 6–15 | Contractor procures and executes drainage, utilities, support, material trials and construction in suitable weather windows; supervision consultant witnesses hold points; QRDC audits. | G2: test compliance, as-built quantities, verified opening dates and independent construction-cost report. |
| 15–36 | Owners maintain all sections to the same service standard; independent team monitors after major storms, before/after monsoons and quarterly, then updates the economic model for final review. | G3: at least two post-construction monsoons if seasonal timing permits; otherwise monitoring extension. |
| At month 36 or after monitoring extension | Independent team and sponsor publish data, uncertainty and technical peer review. | G4: conditional scale-up by road class; retain 5–10-year follow-up and performance contract data. |

**Measure instead of assume:** axle spectra and cumulative load; initial and repeated IRI; asphalt rut depth and crack area; concrete crack severity, joint faulting and spalling; drainage blockage/ponding duration; unplanned closure hours; construction delay vehicle-hours; repair interventions and paid cost; verified local/imported input shares; fuel, electricity and material quantities for emissions; safety exposure and incidents. Weather and maintenance logs are essential covariates.

**Proposed decision targets:** 100% of required acceptance records available; no section opened before contractual conditions are met; no untreated safety-critical defect; all drain outlets accepted before surfacing; publish every planned quarterly dataset. Set roughness/rut/faulting and intervention limits in the protocol from design and applicable specifications, before outcomes are known. Prefer a segment treatment if it meets these gates and retains an economic advantage under predeclared sensitivity cases; otherwise report no decisive winner and continue evidence collection. Do not fabricate a universal target such as “40-year life” from two monsoons.

## Remaining information required for a bankable scheme

Actual road locations, classified axle survey, wet-season geotechnical and drainage investigations, utility plans, current delivered BOQ rates, contractor productivity, permitted traffic diversions, maintenance response capacity and material environmental data. These determine whether the preferred policy produces savings at any specific Nepal site. Screening costs should remain ranges until these are collected.
